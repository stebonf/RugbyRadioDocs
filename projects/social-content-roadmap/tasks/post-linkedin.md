## 07/22/2026

From Events to Stories

Most AI applications start with text.
Rugby Radio Live doesn't.

It starts with structured events.
A try.
A penalty.
A substitution.
A scrum.
A line-out.

Each tap made by the commentator becomes a structured event that carries meaning.

The AI doesn't need to guess what happened.
It already knows.

Its job is to transform those events into a story that sounds natural for the people following the match.

That design choice changes everything.
Instead of asking an LLM to invent a commentary from scratch, the platform provides context, structure and chronology.

The result is more consistent, easier to control and far closer to how a real commentator builds a narrative.

Sometimes the quality of AI doesn't depend on choosing a bigger model.
It depends on giving the model better information.

## 23/06/2026

Event-Driven Architecture Behind Rugby Radio Live

Most people see a rugby match.

I see a stream of events.
Every try, penalty, substitution, yellow card, or score update becomes a structured event inside Rugby Radio Live.
That event doesn't just update a scoreboard.

It can trigger:
🏉 Live commentary
📊 Match statistics
📰 Post-match articles
🎙️ Audio content
📱 Notifications
📈 Analytics

The interesting challenge was designing a system where a single action performed by a commentator can power multiple experiences for different audiences.

The platform relies heavily on event-driven principles.
Instead of building separate workflows for every feature, the match event becomes the source of truth.

Once the event exists, different services can react to it independently.
This approach made Rugby Radio Live far more scalable than the original idea I had when I started the project.

Sometimes the most important architectural decision isn't the technology you choose.

It's choosing the right thing to model.

## 19/06/2026

Why Multiple AI Personalities Matter

One of the first questions people ask about Rugby Radio Live is:

"Why create multiple AI characters instead of using a single assistant?"

The short answer is simple:

Because different roles require different perspectives.

A rugby commentator focuses on the action.

A reporter focuses on the story.

An artist focuses on visual content.

An analyst focuses on requirements.

An architect focuses on system design.

A DevOps engineer focuses on reliability.

Humans naturally switch context between these roles.

I wanted to explore what happens when AI does the same.

Instead of building a single generic assistant, Rugby Radio Live is evolving into a team of specialized AI members, each with its own role, personality and objectives.

The interesting part isn't the technology itself.

It's how specialization changes the quality of the outcome.

Just like in software teams, not every problem should be solved by the same person.

Maybe the same applies to AI.

When people first hear about Rugby Radio Live, they often assume it requires a complex setup.

They simply select what happened on the field:

🟨 Yellow Card

Behind that simple action, Rugby Radio Live transforms structured match events into live updates, AI-generated commentary, match statistics, and post-game content.

The goal was never to build another sports platform.
The goal was to make live match storytelling accessible to anyone standing on the sidelines.

Because sometimes the best technology is the one that disappears behind a simple button.

## 6/9/2026

Why I Built Rugby Radio Live

Two years ago, I wasn't trying to build an AI platform.
I was just a dad standing on the sideline of a rugby field.

My two sons play amateur rugby, and like many parents, I couldn't always be there for every match. Sometimes work got in the way. Sometimes logistics did.

I started wondering:
"What if anyone could follow a rugby match in real time, even from hundreds of kilometers away?"

That simple question became Rugby Radio Live.

What started as an idea to help families stay connected to amateur rugby slowly evolved into something much bigger: a platform that combines event-driven architecture, AI-generated commentary, automated content creation, and digital fan engagement.

Today, Rugby Radio Live can transform match events into live commentary, post-match reports, blog articles, and social content.

The project has become a personal laboratory where I can experiment with software architecture, AI workflows, local models, automation, and product development.

But the original goal hasn't changed.

Making rugby more accessible for the people who care about it.
This page will share the journey behind the project: the architecture, the technology, the lessons learned, and the experiments that work (and those that don't).

Thanks for following along.
